# Theoretical Framework

This document explains and justifies the theoretical choices built into GTVH Multimodal Analyzer.

## 1. Framework

The annotation scheme applies the General Theory of Verbal Humour (Attardo & Raskin 1991; Attardo 2001, 2020) to cartoons, following its multimodal extension by Tsakona (2009).

- **Basis.** The GTVH revises Raskin's (1985) Semantic Script Theory of Humour. A text is humorous if it is compatible, fully or in part, with two scripts that are opposed (Raskin 1985).
- **Six KRs.** The GTVH adds five knowledge resources (KRs) to the Script Opposition: Logical Mechanism, Situation, Target, Narrative Strategy and Language. The KRs are hierarchically ordered, and the hierarchy predicts perceived similarity between jokes (Attardo & Raskin 1991; Ruch, Attardo & Raskin 1993).
- **Cartoons.** The GTVH was developed on verbal jokes but is not limited to verbal humour. Attardo (2020) regrets having named the last KR "Language" rather than "Semiotic Strategy". Tsakona (2009) shows that the KRs, punch and jab lines, and hyperdetermination remain "useful and usable" for cartoons whose humour is verbal, visual, or produced by the interaction of the two (2009).

The GTVH is a theory of the humorous competence in Chomskyan sense (Attardo 2020). The annotation therefore describes how a cartoon's humour is constructed. It does not judge whether the cartoon is funny, how readers respond, or whether its message is right.

## 2. What is annotated

| KR / code | annotated | basis |
|---|---|---|
| Script Opposition (SO) | yes, at three levels | Raskin 1985; Attardo et al. 2002; Attardo 2001, 2020 |
| Logical Mechanism (LM) | no | see §3 |
| Situation (SI) | yes | Attardo 2020 |
| Target (TA) | yes | Attardo 2020 |
| Narrative Strategy (NS) | yes | Attardo 2001; Tsakona 2009 |
| Language (LA) | yes, multimodal | Tsakona 2009; Attardo 2020 |
| Presuppositions, Theme | supplementary codes, not KRs | §9 |

## 3. Logical Mechanism

The LM is the playful, partial resolution of the incongruity (Attardo 2020). It is not annotated, for two reasons.

1. **Its taxonomy is open and disputed.** Attardo et al. (2002) list some 27 mechanisms and do not present the list as closed.
2. **It would cost too much to do reliably.** A reliable operationalization would need a validated coding scheme for every mechanism, which would require a separate study.

## 4. Script Opposition

**Overlap and opposition.** An SO requires two scripts that the cartoon is compatible with (overlap) and that are opposed. Neither condition alone produces humour (Raskin 1985; Attardo 2001).

- Opposed scripts negate each other along a shared axis, a "conceptual integrator".
- Lexical antonyms use the default axis. Local antonyms use an axis that the context forces: two terms that are not opposites in general become opposites in this discourse.
- Terms that share no axis are not opposed.

The annotator is therefore told to name the axis along which the scripts are opposed.

**Three levels.**

1. **Concrete** SO: specific to the cartoon. It is identified first, because analysts who start from the abstract level go wrong (Attardo 2020).
2. **Intermediate** SO: introduced to bridge the concrete and abstract levels
   - It is recorded as a **free label**, not chosen from a fixed list. Attardo notes that the lower-level oppositions vary across cultures while the abstract types do not (2001). He also notes that "any number of intermediate scripts" may bridge the concrete and abstract levels (2020). A fixed list would impose one study's categories on every corpus.
   - Where a basic opposition fits, its usual wording is used (good/bad, life/death, obscene/non-obscene, money/no-money, high/low stature; Raskin 1985). This helps the labels converge across a corpus.
3. **Abstract** SO: one of Raskin's three types of the real/unreal contrast, actual/non-actual, normal/abnormal and possible/impossible (Raskin 1985).
   - Borderline cases between types are recorded as alternatives, since the types are not sharply bounded.

**Backgrounded incongruities.** These are incongruities built into the text world and accepted without question, such as talking animals or stereotyped traits. They belong to the Situation, not to the SO, unless the text focalizes them (Hempelmann & Attardo 2011; Attardo 2020).

## 5. Hyperdetermination

**Instances.** The GTVH analyses each instance of humour, whether punch line or jab line, with its own SO (Attardo 2001; Tsakona 2003). A cartoon may contain several (Tsakona 2009: §5.2.2).

**Hyperdetermination.** Attardo (2001) distinguishes two kinds, as summarised by Tsakona (2009):

- **textual:** several active sources of humour at once;
- **punctual:** one source active at several levels.

**How the scheme operationalizes this.** Annotators tend to treat sharpening detail as a separate joke. The Stage 1 instructions therefore make the decision depend on evidence, which the model works through before it answers; only the resulting SOs are output.

- The model distinguishes **trigger** elements, which evoke a pair of opposed scripts, from **supporting** elements, which only reinforce or sharpen an opposition.
- An additional SO is recorded only if it passes an **independence test**: with the triggers of the existing SOs set aside, the remaining elements must evoke their own overlapping, opposed scripts. This covers textual hyperdetermination.
- A second opposition evoked by the same element belongs to the SO of that element. Punctual hyperdetermination is therefore not recorded separately.

## 6. Situation

The Situation is the background macro-script in which the events take place. It may be activated entirely by inference (Attardo 2020), and it holds the backgrounded incongruities (§4). In a multi-panel cartoon, one macro-script is coded for the whole cartoon.

## 7. Target

**Targets.** The Target is the butt of the joke: a person, group, institution, or a practice or ideology (Attardo 2020). Targets are recorded as the specific referents attacked. The Target is optional (Attardo 2020).

**Orientation First.** Following Priego-Valverde et al. (2018, as discussed in Attardo 2020), the scheme first records the humour's orientation: first person, second person, third party, or setting. Only an orientation with aggression yields a target. This avoids treating every depicted participant as a butt.

## 8. Narrative Strategy and Language/Semiotic Strategy

**Narrative Strategy.** The visual humour genre (editorial cartoon, comic strip, meme), the panel layout, and delivery devices such as dialogue, caption, label and visual allegory all belong to the Narrative Strategy. Attardo confirmed this assignment in personal correspondence (S. Attardo, personal communication, September 11, 2026). Accordingly the scheme records:

- the genre, set once per project because it is constant within a corpus;
- layout and panel count;
- delivery devices such as dialogue, caption, label, signage and visual allegory;
- the distribution of humour as punch and jab lines in reading order (Attardo 2001).

**Language/Semiotic Strategy.** The Language KR is extended to "any kind of codification appearing in the text" (Tsakona 2009). For each SO the scheme records:

- **Locus**, using the three cartoon types of Samson and Huber (2007), also used by Tsakona (2009):
  - verbal only;
  - verbal–visual interaction;
  - visual only.
- **Interaction dynamic**: enhancing, complementary, counterpointing or contradictory (Nikolajeva & Scott 2000, as applied by Tsakona 2009).
- **Semiotic mechanisms**, including **visual metaphor** with its target and source domains (El Refaie 2003; Forceville 2002).

## 9. Supplementary Codes and Corpus Coding

**Presuppositions and Theme** are not GTVH knowledge resources. Presuppositions record the background knowledge a reading requires. The theme records the topic. Both are labelled as supplementary codes.

**Corpus Coding (Stage 4).** The GTVH describes individual texts. Generalizing over a corpus needs categories, so the scheme separates two layers:

- **Interpretation (Stages 1–3):** free-text targets, themes and intermediate oppositions, as the theory defines them.
- **Categorization (Stage 4):** the free-text labels are coded into the categories of a project codebook.

The codebook can be developed deductively, from the literature or the research questions, or inductively, from the annotations themselves. These are the two standard routes of content analysis (Krippendorff 2004).

Keeping the codebook out of Stages 1–3 prevents the categories from priming the interpretation. It also lets the codebook be revised without re-annotating the cartoons.
